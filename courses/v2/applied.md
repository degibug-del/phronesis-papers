# Applied Framework: Your Life

*by Diego Rincón · phronesis.world*

You will finish this course with a written map of your own displacement — where you have drifted, what staying there costs you per day, what going back would cost once — and a 90-day plan for the one or two returns that matter most.

## Who this is for

This is the practical companion to the framework. The theory in brief: every system has a ground state S0 — the state it was built for, the configuration it would hold if nothing were pulling on it. It also has an actual state S*, which is where it actually is. The gap between them is displacement, written xi. Displacement charges rent: a daily cost, D(xi), that compounds quietly because each day's installment is small enough to ignore. Returning to S0 also costs something — a one-time price, C_return. And the maintenance theorem says that returning on a schedule beats letting displacement accumulate, because drift grows over time while the fixed cost of a return stays roughly fixed.

That is the whole machine. This course points it at your life.

It is for you if you suspect, without being able to prove it, that your days do not match your intentions. If you are competent, busy, mostly functional, and quietly aware that the life you are running is not the one you would design. The audit will turn that suspicion into sentences, the sentences into a decision, and the decision into a plan.

It is not for you if you want motivation. There is none here. There is accounting, and then there is work.

## Module 1: The Method

### The five quantities

For each of eight domains — health, attention, work, love, society, technology, money, ecology — you will write down five things. One sentence each. The whole audit fits on two pages.

**S0 — your ground state.** Not a fantasy. S0 is not "billionaire with a six-pack." It is the state this domain would hold if nothing were pulling it off course: the configuration that matches your own stated intentions, the version you would defend without notes. A useful test: S0 should sound boring to anyone but you. "I sleep enough and my energy is stable" is a ground state. "I run ultramarathons" is a goal, which is a different thing and not your problem right now.

**S* — your actual state.** Described flatly, the way a stranger would report it. Not how you feel about it, not the trend, not the excuse. What is.

**xi — the displacement.** The specific, nameable gap. Not "I'm unhealthy" but "I sleep six broken hours and haven't moved with intent in a month." Vague displacement cannot be costed. Specific displacement can.

**D(xi) — the daily cost.** What staying displaced takes from you per day: energy, money, mood, attention, optionality, closeness. Write the honest line item, even when it is uncomfortable. Especially then.

**C_return — the return cost.** What going back to S0 would cost, paid once: time, discomfort, money, conversations you have been avoiding, an identity you would have to put down.

### Why daily cost beats willpower

Most self-improvement runs on willpower aimed at outcomes. It fails on schedule, because willpower is a reserve and outcomes are far away. This audit runs on accounting instead, and accounting does not require motivation. It requires looking.

The reason to write D(xi) down is that displacement hides its rent. Each day's cost is small enough to wave off; the sum is not. As a toy model: suppose a displacement costs you the equivalent of one focused hour per day — lost to fatigue, scrolling, low-grade dread, whatever form the rent takes. Over a year that is the better part of two working months, gone, with nothing to show for it. Nobody would sign that contract if it were printed. You signed it by not looking.

C_return has the opposite distortion. It looks bigger than it is, because it is all due at once and most of it is denominated in discomfort. A hard conversation, three bad weeks of withdrawal, an evening with bank statements. The audit's job is to put D(xi) and C_return on the same page so the comparison is fair for the first time. Run the comparison on your own numbers and you will often find that a few months of rent exceeds the entire return cost — that you have been paying the return cost many times over, just slowly, and for nothing.

### Rules of an honest audit

1. **Describe, don't judge.** The audit records states, not verdicts about your character. "I check my phone before my feet touch the floor" is data. "I'm pathetic" is noise. Noise corrupts the data and stops the audit.
2. **One sentence per quantity.** If you need a paragraph, you are explaining. Explanation is where the truth goes to get negotiated down.
3. **No averaging across domains.** A strong work life does not offset an absent one in love. Each domain is audited alone. The whole point is to see where the displacement actually lives, not to compute a grade you can live with.
4. **"Conscious stay" is a legitimate verdict.** Some displacements are worth their rent for a season. The framework does not demand you return everywhere. It demands you stop pretending you haven't decided.
5. **Don't fix anything yet.** The urge to act mid-audit is the urge to stop looking. Finish the page first.

### Practice

This week: pick any one of the eight domains and write its five quantities. Paper, five sentences, ten minutes. Do not pick the easiest one or the hardest one — pick the one you thought of first when you read the list.

- Which quantity was hardest to write, S0 or S*? What does that tell you?
- Did D(xi) change size once it was a sentence instead of a feeling?
- If a friend handed you this page about their own life, what would you say to them?

## Module 2: The Audit — Body and Mind

### Health

The ground state here is older than you. An animal at rest sleeps when tired, wakes without violence, moves daily because stillness feels wrong, eats food that was recently alive in the ground, and runs on energy that holds steady across the day. Your S0 is some version of that, adjusted for your actual body and circumstances — and you know roughly what it is, because you have visited it. Most people have had at least one stretch, even a short one, when the machine simply worked. Write that down. That memory is your S0.

Now S*. Common displacements, reported flatly: sleep compressed and borrowed against with caffeine; movement outsourced to intentions; meals assembled from packages and timed by stress; a body managed for symptoms rather than lived in. The signal that you are displaced is not pain — pain comes late. It is that you treat your body as a logistics problem.

D(xi) for health is the upstream tax: it is charged to every other domain before you spend a single hour in any of them. Tired is dumber, more irritable, more impulsive, less generous. You pay health rent in bad work, short tempers, and skipped evenings, and the bill never says "sleep" on it.

C_return is mostly discomfort spread over weeks, and it is cheaper than it looks. Sleep is the cheapest entry: a fixed wake time, kept even on weekends, drags everything else into line behind it. Movement: walking counts; the bar for "moved today" should be low enough to clear on your worst day. Food: shift the default plate toward things that grew — oats, beans, lentils, rice, greens, fruit, nuts. You do not need a system. You need a default that requires no decision.

This is a framework for thinking, not medical advice — work with a clinician on real conditions.

### Attention

S0: you decide what you think about. You can hold one thread for an hour. Boredom is an input — the feeling that precedes an idea — not an emergency to be patched with a screen. Your attention goes where you point it and stays until you call it back.

S*, for most people now: the phone as reflex rather than tool; a background hum of unfinished feeds; the inability to watch a film, read a chapter, or wait in a line without reaching for the device. The cleanest displacement signal: you check your phone while a page is loading on your laptop. You are not seeking information. You are avoiding a half-second of stillness.

D(xi) here is special, and worse than it looks, because attention displacement is a multiplier on every other cost. It does not just take its own hours — it degrades the hours that remain. Work done in fragments takes longer and comes out worse. Conversations held at half-presence don't bind. A walk taken with a podcast and three check-ins is not the walk your nervous system needed. This is why attention sits second in the return sequence, right after health: until you can hold a thread, you cannot execute any other return.

C_return: physical distance from the device (another room is a strategy; a pocket is not), a deliberately boring phone, a few days of genuine withdrawal-grade discomfort, and the small social friction of answering slower than people expect. That friction fades. People recalibrate to your new latency, and the ones who matter never minded.

### Practice

This week: for one ordinary day, log two things only — when you sleep and wake, and a tally mark for every time you pick up your phone without a specific purpose. Then write the five quantities for health and for attention, using that day as evidence rather than memory.

- Where did the tally marks cluster, and what were you avoiding in those moments?
- Whose interests does your current S* serve, if not yours?
- What would the first morning of S0 look like, concretely, from the second your eyes open?

## Module 3: The Audit — Work and Love

### Work

S0 for work is not a dream job. It is alignment: work that uses your actual capacities on problems you would choose, at a pace that does not strip-mine the other seven domains, in exchange for enough. Most people have never written this sentence for themselves, which is why their work S* is graded against a fantasy or against nothing.

Common displacements: the competence trap — you became excellent at something you stopped caring about, and now the excellence is the cage; prestige-chasing, where the job is a costume worn for an audience that isn't watching; and expansion, where work fills every hour because nothing else in your life currently has the standing to object.

The displacement signal is Sunday evening. Notice what arrives. Mild readiness is alignment. Dread is data.

D(xi) for work is paid in identity and in leakage. You spend the largest block of your waking hours becoming someone — displaced work means becoming someone you didn't choose, daily. And the resentment doesn't stay at the office; it leaks into evenings and gets billed to the people you live with.

C_return is usually the largest of the eight, and you should say so honestly rather than flinch. It can mean a conversation that changes how you're seen, a boundary that costs a promotion, a skill rebuilt at night over a year, a smaller title, a smaller number. Two things make it payable. First, it can almost always be staged — paid in installments rather than at once. Second, the size of C_return is exactly why the verdict must be conscious: a high return cost can justify "scheduled return" or even "conscious stay for this season." It cannot justify never deciding. Never deciding is just paying D(xi) forever and calling it patience.

### Love

S0: relationships in which you are known — partner, friends, family, in whatever configuration is yours — with attention flowing both directions and presence as the default. Not constant harmony. Known.

S*, in common forms: a partnership that has quietly become a logistics company run by two tired colleagues; friendships conducted entirely through read receipts and "we should catch up"; calling your mother when guilt finally outweighs avoidance. The displacement signal: you give people updates instead of letting them see states. You report your life to the people who are supposed to be in it.

D(xi) here is the heaviest rent in the audit, and the best hidden. Loneliness inside relationships weighs more than loneliness alone, because it forecloses the obvious remedy. And conflict avoided long enough stops being conflict and becomes distance — which feels like peace and costs like grief.

C_return: vulnerability, conversations with unscripted outcomes, apologies that don't immediately get accepted, and time spent in ways that do not scale and cannot be optimized. That last part is the point. The inefficiency is the payload.

### Practice

This week: write the five quantities for work and for love. Then have one ten-minute conversation you have been deferring — not the hardest one on the list. The cheapest one. Pay the smallest C_return you can find and notice what it actually cost versus what you had budgeted in dread.

- In work: are you displaced from your ground state, or have you simply never written down what S0 is? These need different responses.
- Which relationship has the lowest C_return relative to its daily cost?
- What are you currently pretending is a work problem that is actually a love problem — or the reverse?

## Module 4: The Audit — World and Means

### Society

S0: a place in some human web where your presence is noticed and your absence would be — neighbors who know your name, a few rooms where you have standing, some civic surface area you chose on purpose. Belonging at human scale.

S*: opinions instead of neighbors. A feed full of positions on everything and a street full of strangers. Community replaced by audience — and an audience, however large, does not bring soup when you're sick.

D(xi) is paid in fragility and in noise: no local web to catch you when something breaks, and a head full of distant conflicts you can't affect, displacing the near ones you could. C_return is mostly repeated, slightly awkward showing up — the same faces, the same places, until familiarity compounds. It is embarrassingly cheap and almost universally deferred.

### Technology

This one is distinct from attention. Attention is your mind; technology is the inventory — the actual stack of machines, apps, accounts, and services your life runs on. S0: tools you chose, that do a job and stop when you stop, that you could each name and justify. The tool waits for you.

S*: a stack assembled by defaults and vendors. Apps you don't remember installing, accounts you can't list, services that summon you on their schedule, upgrades accepted because declining took a click more. The displacement signal: you could not write down your own inventory from memory.

D(xi): a thousand small summonses, plus the ambient sense that your tools have opinions about your time. C_return is genuinely low — an afternoon of inventory and deletion, defaults reset to silence, notifications reduced to things that are actually about you. Few returns in this course buy more relief per hour spent.

### Money

S0: money as stored optionality. You know what comes in, what goes out, what you have, and how long it would hold you — and your spending roughly matches what you actually value, rather than what you reflexively reach for. Note what S0 is not: it is not a particular number. It is the absence of fog.

S*: the fog itself. Not necessarily poverty — plenty of high earners live here — but unknowing. Statements unopened, totals unfaced, lifestyle drifting upward to meet income, payments structured by design so that you never see a sum. The displacement signal: you cannot state, within a rough band, what last month cost you.

D(xi) is paid in background fear and in surrendered decisions: you cannot decide about what you cannot see, so the defaults decide for you, daily. As a toy model: suppose ten small automatic payments each feel like nothing on their own. The audit's job is to make them one visible number, because ten invisible nothings sum to one real something every month, indefinitely.

C_return: a few honest hours with statements, the discomfort of seeing totals, possibly a deliberately smaller life for a while. The fear of looking is almost always worse than the looking.

This is a framework for thinking, not financial advice — for real decisions about debt, investing, or taxes, work with a qualified professional.

### Ecology

S0: you live somewhere, not just at an address. Daylight on your skin most days, some awareness of season and weather as things that happen to you rather than backdrop, walking as transport at least sometimes, and a material footprint you would endorse if you saw it itemized.

S*: the climate-controlled loop — house to car to building to car to house — with nature consumed as content between stops. The displacement signal: you cannot remember the last time you were outside for an hour with no device and no destination.

D(xi) is subtle and physiological: a nervous system that never downshifts, senses tuned to screens and dulled to everything else. C_return is nearly zero, which makes this the strangest entry in the audit — the rent is real and the return is a door. Outside is free and has no onboarding.

### Practice

This week: write the five quantities for all four of these domains. Then run one concrete probe: list every recurring charge you pay, from memory, on paper. Then pull your statements and compare. The gap between the two lists is your money displacement, made visible in twenty minutes.

- Which of these four felt least like yours to even audit — and who taught you that?
- What did the memory-versus-statement gap show you?
- If you vanished from your neighborhood tomorrow, who outside your household would notice — and how long would it take?

## Module 5: Deciding — Stay or Return

### Three verdicts

You now have, or will shortly have, eight small five-line audits. Every domain gets exactly one of three verdicts.

**Return now.** The displacement is costing too much to carry and this is its season. You will build the 90-day plan in Module 6 around it. You may issue this verdict to at most two domains. One is better.

**Scheduled return.** Real displacement, real rent, but not now — because another return is in progress, or because C_return depends on something with its own timeline. A scheduled return gets a date written next to it. A date, not a vibe.

**Conscious stay.** The displacement is worth its rent for this season — a demanding job that funds a return elsewhere, a hard year of caring for someone, a deliberate trade you would defend out loud. Legitimate, and necessary: pretending you'll fix everything is how you fix nothing. But a conscious stay gets an expiry date, written down, at which you re-audit. A conscious stay without an expiry date is denial with paperwork.

### Triage: one return, maybe two

You cannot return in eight domains at once. Every C_return draws on the same reserves — attention, discomfort tolerance, hours — and splitting them eight ways means eight failures that teach you the false lesson that returning doesn't work. It works. It just doesn't parallelize.

The naive rule is ratio: return first where daily cost is highest relative to return cost. That rule is right between equals. But the domains are not equals, which is why sequence overrides ratio.

### Sequencing: health, attention, work, love

**Health first.** Every other return is paid for in energy, and health is where energy comes from. Attempting a work return while sleeping six broken hours is attempting it with borrowed money at bad rates. Even if health is not your worst displacement, a minimal health floor — fixed wake time, daily movement, plants on the plate — is the enabling move for everything downstream.

**Attention second.** Every return in this course requires holding a thread for weeks. A shattered attention span cannot execute a 90-day plan; it cannot execute a 90-minute one. Reclaim the instrument before pointing it at anything.

**Work third.** It is the largest structural block of your waking time, it funds the material side of every other return, and its displacement leaks into all of them. With energy restored and attention intact, you can finally see your work clearly enough to issue an honest verdict on it — many people discover their work audit changes after the first two returns.

**Love fourth — not because it matters least, but because it is where you arrive.** Love returns are made of presence, and presence is precisely what the first three restore. Arriving at the hard conversation depleted, distracted, and resentful sabotages it. Arriving rested and present, you may find half the displacement was never about the relationship at all.

The other four slot in around this spine, and mostly piggyback: technology rides with attention (same afternoon, often), money rides with work, ecology rides with health, society rides with love. You rarely need to schedule them separately — you need to notice the coupling and let one return pull its partner.

### Practice

This week: issue all eight verdicts. Write them in the worksheet at the end of this course. Circle one "return now" — two only if they genuinely couple, like attention and technology. Put real dates on every scheduled return and every conscious stay.

- Did you give any domain "conscious stay" to avoid the work? Reread that one slowly.
- Which return are you most drawn to — and is that because it is most needed, or least frightening?
- What expiry dates did you write, and where will you record them so future-you actually encounters them?

## Module 6: The 90-Day Return

### Why 90 days

Long enough for a structure to harden into a default; short enough to hold the whole arc in view from day one. The plan is deliberately modest: not transformation, not a new you. One return, executed, then maintained. That's the product.

The plan has three phases. Each phase has one goal. Refusing to do the later phases' work early is most of the discipline.

### Weeks 1–2: Strip and measure

Goal: establish the baseline and remove what feeds the displacement. You are not building anything yet.

Two moves. First, measure: log the relevant raw facts daily — sleep times, pickups, spending, hours, whatever your domain's tally is. Crude is fine; honest is mandatory. Second, strip: remove the two or three things most obviously feeding the displacement. The apps off the phone. The packaged defaults out of the kitchen, a pot of beans and a bag of oats in. The standing meeting declined. Subtraction before addition, always — addition on top of an unstripped life is how plans die in week three.

Also in these two weeks: define your **minimum viable return** — the smallest daily action that touches S0. Fifteen minutes or less. A walk. A page. A single line in a ledger. Phone in the other room from nine o'clock. It should be small enough to survive your worst day, because your worst day is coming and the plan must not die there.

### Weeks 3–6: Build the loop

Goal: convert the action into structure. Willpower got you through the strip phase; it is now retired.

Anchor the minimum viable return to a fixed point that already exists — after waking, after lunch, before the commute home. Same time, same trigger, every day. Track misses without drama: a miss is data about the structure, not about your character. One miss means nothing; the same miss twice means the anchor is wrong — move the anchor, not the standard.

By week six the minimum version should feel mildly boring. That is the success condition. Boredom is what structure feels like from the inside.

### Weeks 7–12: Load and lock

Goal: test the structure under real load, then schedule its maintenance forever.

Life will stress the loop without your help — travel, a deadline, someone getting sick. If it hasn't by week seven, stress it yourself: keep the practice through a deliberately hard week. Watch what breaks, and fix the structure, not yourself. Every failure is a design problem with a design solution: smaller minimum, earlier anchor, fewer dependencies.

Then, before day 90, the most important move in the course: schedule the maintenance.

### The maintenance theorem, lived

You will drift again. This is not a flaw in you or in the plan; drift is what living systems do under load. The theorem says: do not wait for displacement to announce itself, because by then it is expensive. Return on a schedule, at a frequency where the fixed cost of checking is small compared to the drift you would otherwise accumulate between checks.

In practice, three nested loops. A daily check: a few minutes, often just the minimum viable return itself plus one honest glance at the log. A weekly review: half an hour with the week's data — where did S* drift, what does next week need. A quarterly re-audit: the full worksheet, all eight domains, fresh sentences.

As a toy model: suppose the weekly review costs you half an hour, and at one week's drift, half an hour is enough to correct course completely. Skipping ten reviews does not save you five hours. It lets xi grow past the size a half hour can fix, and now you are facing a full C_return again — the very bill this whole course exists to stop you from running up. Frequent small returns are cheaper than rare large ones. That is the entire theorem, applied.

## The Path

The audit is not an event. You have now done it once, and the first time is the hardest — the sentences resist being written because they have been protected by vagueness for years. From here, the framework lives in three places: the daily check, which keeps today's drift small; the weekly review, which catches what the days missed; and the quarterly worksheet, which catches what the weeks missed.

Notice what this means: you are never finished, and you are never starting over. Both of those are displacement stories. The truth is plainer. There is a ground state. There is where you are. There is a gap, a rent, and a price of return. You now know how to read all four, in any domain, for the rest of your life.

The course ends. The practice is daily. Go check the gap.
