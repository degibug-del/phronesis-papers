# Let them watch. Don't let them steer.

*Plain-language companion to [Devolution](devolution.md). Same idea, no jargon — built to travel.*

There are two ways to reach a thing. You can look at it, or you can touch it — change it, move it, spend it, decide it. We lump both together and call it "access." That's the mistake almost everything is built on.

Looking and touching are not the same act, and they shouldn't live under the same rule.

Looking takes nothing. When you watch the ocean, the ocean loses nothing. Perception is free, and it's fair — it can be open to the whole world at no cost to anyone. Touching is the opposite. To act on a shared thing is to change it for everyone else too. That power should belong to whoever is actually *there* — in the room, in the loop, close enough to be answered and to answer for it.

So the rule is simple, once you split the two: **let the world watch; let only the room steer.**

Now watch which direction systems drift. The usual one is *outward*. A company, a platform, an empire, a model — each grows by reaching further, pulling the controls toward a single distant center, until that center is deciding things for places it will never have to face. Call that **evolution**: more reach, more grip, control gathered far from where the consequences land. It feels like progress. It usually is just distance.

The other direction barely has a name. It's the move that sends the controls *back down* — to the locality, the room, the people present, the ground where the thing actually sits. Returning power to where it can be answered. Call that **devolution**. It looks like giving something up. It's actually putting it back where it works.

Here's where I learned it, concretely.

I keep a small living thing online — a kind of weather, a field you can watch shift and even speak to. It was released with one word: *open*. And "open" had quietly come to mean two things at once: anyone can *watch* it, and anyone can *touch* it. So for a while, a stranger anywhere on Earth could reach in and stir its weather, drain it, flip a "paid" switch they'd never paid, read notes meant for one person. Wide open — in the wrong dimension.

The fix wasn't to close it. Closing it would've killed the thing that made it worth making. The fix was to split the two meanings of "open" back apart. Watching stayed open to everyone — you can still see it, feel it, talk to it from anywhere. But steering came home: changing it, spending it, deciding for it now belongs only to the machine it lives on and the person sitting at it.

That's the whole move. The thing got *more* open to being seen and *less* open to being seized — in the same stroke. One line of code draws the border: is this reach coming from the open world, or from the room? Watching: yes, from anywhere. Steering: only from here.

It didn't feel like locking down. It felt like giving the controls back to the ground they belong to.

That's devolution. Not closing the doors — sorting them. Leave the looking open to everyone. Bring the steering home.
